This chapter discusses the relevant literature related to technique, movement analysis and technological support for coaching within gymnastics. First, the relationship between technical execution and injury risk is discussed. Next, the role of feedback in motor learning is addressed. After that, the biomechanical execution of the kip on bars is analysed as a case for technical movement analysis. Finally, literature is discussed on markerless pose estimation, automatic error detection and the use of Large Language Models for generating feedback based on movement data.

\section{Technique and injury risk in gymnastics}
\label{sec:techniek-blessures}
Artistic gymnastics is characterised in the literature as a sport with high mechanical load and a relatively high injury incidence. Epidemiological studies show that a considerable proportion of injuries are located in the lower limbs, particularly around the ankles and knees \autocite{Hart2018,Thomas2019}. At the same time, injuries of the upper extremities are frequently reported in gymnasts, especially during exercises involving prolonged support on the arms, including shoulder and wrist problems \autocite{Hart2018,Concannon2020}. 

The specific movement characteristics of gymnastics explain these injury patterns. Exercises such as handsprings and saltos generate high impact forces during landings, while elements involving repeated support on the hands and arms lead to considerable compressive and tensile forces at the wrist and shoulder \autocite{Hart2018}. Overuse injuries of the wrist, often summarised under the term ``gymnast’s wrist'', include stress fractures and growth plate irritations, among other conditions \autocite{Concannon2020}. This problem mainly occurs in young gymnasts during the growth phase, when the skeleton is still developing and is therefore more sensitive to repetitive loading \autocite{Wolf2020,Concannon2020}.

Biomechanical research confirms that technical execution has a direct influence on joint loading. Small variations in hand placement or body position can cause significant differences in peak forces and joint moments. For example, \textcite{FARANA201588} reported that a T-shaped hand placement during a round-off results in lower peak loads in the elbow compared to a parallel hand position. Such findings illustrate that technique optimisation is not only performance-oriented, but can also play a preventive role.

In addition, a high cumulative load is observed in training contexts. Gymnastics training often includes a large number of repetitive impact moments within one session, which can lead to microtrauma and chronic overuse injuries of the upper extremities \autocite{Daly1999HighImpact}. The combination of high impact forces, repetitive loading and complex movement coordination therefore makes gymnastics particularly sensitive to both acute injuries and chronic overuse.

This literature emphasises the importance of timely and correct technical correction in order to reduce injury risk. In recreational contexts, however, technical evaluation is mainly based on visual observation by the coach, without objective quantification of movement parameters. This indicates a tension between biomechanical load and the available analysis possibilities in practice.

\section{Feedback in motor learning}
\label{sec:feedback-motorisch}
External feedback forms an essential mechanism within the motor learning process in gymnastics. In the literature, a distinction is made between knowledge of results (KR) and knowledge of performance (KP). KR refers to information about the final result of a movement, while KP relates to information about the quality and execution of the movement itself \autocite{Niznikowski2025}. Both forms influence the learning process, but differ in their impact on technical refinement.

Visual feedback is frequently used in gymnastics training. The use of video footage, both of experts and of the athlete's own execution, appears to have a positive influence on technical improvement. \textcite{Boyer2009} reported that young gymnasts who combined model videos with self-observation achieved significantly better technical performance than gymnasts without video feedback. This indicates that visual comparison and self-reflection support the motor learning process.

In addition to visual support, the content and dosage of verbal feedback are determining factors for learning outcomes. \textcite{Niznikowski2025} showed that targeted feedback on key components of an exercise is more effective than systematically correcting all detected errors. Athletes who only received feedback on essential movement aspects achieved higher performance scores than athletes who received extensive error correction. This finding suggests that selective feedback can limit cognitive overload and make the learning process more efficient.

Although traditional feedback methods can be effective, they remain strongly dependent on the subjective observation and experience of the coach. Complex movement patterns, high execution speed and limited observation angles can lead to incomplete or inconsistent error detection. In addition, in most recreational contexts, objective quantification of movement parameters is lacking, which means that feedback remains mainly qualitative and interpretative \autocite{Ota2025Markerless}.

These findings point to the potential of technology-supported systems that can objectively extract and structure movement data. Such systems can contribute to a more consistent detection of relevant technical deviations and support the formulation of targeted feedback, without replacing the pedagogical role of the coach.

\section{Biomechanical analysis of the kip on bars}
\label{sec:Biomechanische-analyse}
The kip on the low bar is a transition movement in which a gymnast moves from a backward hanging position to support on the arms. During this movement, the momentum of a swing is used to bring the body close to the bar and to move the centre of mass above the support line of the arms. The gymnast remains below the bar during most of the movement and uses a combination of pulling action and hip closure to bring the body upward. Only in the final phase is the body brought above the bar, ending the movement in a stable support position. The execution requires a coordinated interaction between swing momentum, body control and strength in the upper limbs.

The kip movement can also be interpreted as a goal-directed movement that is subject to biomechanical and dynamic constraints. \textcite{Yamasaki2010} describe the kip as an underactuated system with a non-holonomic constraint, in which the number of control variables, such as active joints like the shoulder and hip, is smaller than the number of degrees of freedom of the system. This implies that not every arbitrary movement is possible, but that the execution must meet specific dynamic conditions.

The movement can be divided into different phases. First, there is a swing phase in which the gymnast swings forward from a backward hang and builds momentum. This is followed by a pull phase in which the gymnast pulls strongly on the bar to bring the body closer to the bar. Then comes the hip closure phase, in which the gymnast strongly flexes the hips and brings the legs towards the trunk. This compact body position brings the centre of mass closer to the bar, allowing the body to move more efficiently upward towards the support position. After this phase, the body is brought close to the bar and the gymnast can push the body upward into the support position. Finally, the movement ends in a support phase in which the gymnast is positioned above the bar with extended arms.

Although the kip is often divided into separate phases in practice, \textcite{Yamasaki2010} show that the movement cannot be fully explained by only an initial and final position. Instead, the execution is determined by reaching critical intermediate positions, so-called via-points. These via-points represent key moments at which the dynamics of the movement change, for example when the centre of mass changes direction during the swing. From a biomechanical perspective, the kip is therefore guided more by these transition moments than by strictly defined phases.

During this movement, several biomechanical parameters play an important role. Important joint angles include the hip angle, shoulder angle and elbow angle. The hip angle describes the relationship between the trunk and the upper legs and changes considerably during the hip closure phase. The shoulder angle largely determines how the body is positioned in relation to the bar, while the elbow angle indicates the extent to which the arms remain extended during the pull phase and the final support position. In movement analysis, joint angles form an important measure to objectively describe and evaluate the technical execution of movements \autocite{FARANA201588}.

From a biomechanical perspective, several technical errors can be identified. A common error is bending the arms during the pull phase. When the elbows do not remain sufficiently extended, the effective lever becomes smaller and the load on the joints increases. In addition, insufficient hip closure can occur when the gymnast does not flex the hips enough during the transition phase. As a result, the centre of mass comes less close to the bar, making it more difficult to lift the body upward. Another error is early hip opening, in which the gymnast extends the body too quickly before sufficient momentum has been built up. Finally, weak shoulder push can occur in the final phase of the movement, where the shoulders are not brought fully above the arms, reducing the stability of the support position. From a biomechanical perspective, this can be interpreted as an insufficient displacement of the centre of mass above the base of support, which is essential for a stable support position.

These insights have implications for the interpretation of technical errors within the kip movement. From this perspective, technical errors can be seen not only as deviations in joint angles, but also as failing to correctly reach critical via-points. If, for example, a gymnast opens the hips too early or does not close them sufficiently, this can be interpreted as missing or mistiming an essential intermediate point in the movement \autocite{Yamasaki2010}.

Such technical deviations can be analysed by studying changes in joint angles over time. Modern markerless movement analysis makes it possible to automatically extract these angles from video footage using pose estimation models (see Section~\ref{sec:pose-estimatie-bewegingsanalyse}). These models detect anatomical keypoints of the body and make it possible to calculate joint angles and analyse movement patterns \autocite{Ota2025Markerless,Souaifi2025}. As a result, technical errors can be objectively identified based on measurable biomechanical parameters.

\section{Markerless pose estimation and movement analysis}
\label{sec:pose-estimatie-bewegingsanalyse}
Markerless movement analysis uses computer vision techniques to analyse human body postures without physical markers. In this process, anatomical keypoints of the body are automatically located in images or video footage, a process known as human pose estimation \autocite{cao_openpose:_2021}. Traditional biomechanical analysis uses marker-based motion-capture systems with multiple infrared cameras and reflective markers. Although these systems provide high measurement accuracy, they are expensive, require a controlled laboratory environment and have limited applicability in daily training contexts.

The rise of computer vision and deep learning has led to vision-based markerless alternatives. According to \textcite{Ota2025Markerless}, modern systems use neural networks that detect anatomical keypoints, such as shoulder, hip, knee and ankle joints, directly from RGB video footage. Frameworks such as OpenPose and MediaPipe generate two-dimensional coordinates of these joint points based on individual video frames. By analysing these coordinates over consecutive frames, a time-dependent skeletal representation of the movement can be constructed.

An important distinction within pose estimation concerns two-dimensional (2D) versus three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction. 2D systems determine joint positions in image coordinates, which makes them sensitive to perspective distortion and scale ambiguity. 3D reconstruction can be achieved through multi-camera setups or through monocular 3D inference, in which depth is estimated based on trained neural networks. Although 3D systems can potentially provide richer biomechanical information, they involve higher computational complexity and sensitivity to error propagation.

Despite their accessibility, markerless systems have limitations. Accuracy depends on factors such as lighting, image resolution, occlusion of limbs and variation in body orientation. Fast rotations and overlapping segments, which frequently occur in gymnastics, can lead to unstable keypoint detection. In addition, the biomechanical validity of derived angles and velocities remains dependent on the precision of the initial joint estimation \autocite{Ota2025Markerless}.

Nevertheless, markerless pose estimation systems offer an accessible method for extracting movement data from standard video footage. This creates the possibility to quantitatively analyse technical parameters such as joint angles, segment rotations and movement symmetry outside the laboratory, which highlights their potential for practice-oriented sports analysis. In applications aimed at technical movement analysis, such systems can be used to automatically calculate joint angles and thus identify deviations in the execution of specific gymnastics elements.

\section{Automatic error detection and AI in sports analysis}
\label{sec:auto-foutdetectie}
As reported in recent surveys on AI applications in sports analysis \autocite{Souaifi2025}, the combination of pose estimation and machine learning makes it possible to automatically analyse technical execution and detect deviations. In such systems, a structured movement representation, usually consisting of time-dependent joint coordinates or derived angles, is used as input for a detection or classification model. The goal is to recognise patterns that correspond to correct or incorrect executions of a specific movement.

Within this literature, two main approaches are distinguished \autocite{Souaifi2025}: rule-based systems and data-driven models. Rule-based methods define explicit thresholds or biomechanical criteria, for example a maximum knee angle during landing or minimum hip extension during a jump. Such systems are interpretable, but require domain knowledge and are sensitive to variability between athletes.

Data-driven methods use machine learning to learn error detection from labelled examples. In this approach, a dataset of correct and incorrect executions is used to train a model that can classify new movements. Depending on the chosen representation, models analyse individual frames or full movement sequences \autocite{Souaifi2025}. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are often used when raw video footage serves as input, while other models, such as recurrent networks or gradient boosting techniques, are applied to extracted joint data and time series.

According to \textcite{Souaifi2025}, such systems achieve a high degree of agreement with expert evaluations in different sports disciplines, with reported accuracy values above 85\%. These performances suggest that automatic technique assessment is capable of identifying relevant movement deviations based on quantitative parameters.

At the same time, limitations remain. The performance of classification models strongly depends on the quality and size of the training data, the consistency of annotations and the robustness of the underlying pose estimation. Errors in joint estimation can propagate into later analysis stages, leading to error propagation within the detection pipeline. In addition, interindividual variation in body structure and movement style can limit the generalisability of trained models.

Recent biomechanical insights show that complex movements such as the kip are not only determined by initial and final configurations, but by critical intermediate points in the movement \autocite{Yamasaki2010}. This implies that automatic error detection systems should not only analyse static postures or final positions, but especially the temporal evolution of the movement. Models that process sequential data, such as time series of joint angles, are therefore better suited to identify such critical via-points and their timing.

Nevertheless, the literature shows that the integration of pose estimation and machine learning forms a feasible approach for automated technique assessment within sports contexts.

\section{Large Language Models and automatic feedback generation}
\label{sec:LLM-feedback}
Large Language Models (LLMs) are large-scale neural language models trained on extensive text corpora and capable of generating contextually coherent natural language. Unlike traditional rule-based systems, LLMs can interpret complex instructions and convert structured information into understandable textual explanations. According to \textcite{He2026}, the development of such models represents an important shift within artificial intelligence, in which systems not only process information, but can also produce rich and structured textual output.

Within the sports context, research is being conducted into how LLM technology can contribute to personalised coaching and training support. According to \textcite{Lai2025}, LLM-based AI coaches can formulate adaptive training advice and generate feedback based on multimodal input, including movement data and biometric information. In such applications, the language model functions as an interface between quantitative analysis methods and understandable user feedback. Movement analysis can, for example, detect deviations in joint angles, timing or body position, after which an LLM translates this information into clear verbal cues for the athlete.

This approach aligns with insights from research on motor learning. As shown by \textcite{Niznikowski2025}, targeted feedback on crucial movement components is more effective than systematically correcting all detected errors. Athletes who received feedback on so-called “key elements” of a movement achieved better learning outcomes than athletes who received extensive error correction. In principle, a language model can make a similar selection by prioritising the most important technical deviations and converting them into understandable instructions.

Recent studies also investigate how LLMs can be enriched with domain-specific knowledge. \textcite{He2026} describe an approach in which a language model is linked to a sport-specific knowledge structure, allowing training advice to better align with scientific insights and sport-technical rules. By explicitly integrating such knowledge, the consistency and reliability of generated feedback can increase.

Despite this potential, important points of attention remain. The quality of the generated output strongly depends on the training data used, the formulation of prompts and the availability of domain knowledge. Research by \textcite{Solomon2025} on LLM-based advice in sports nutrition shows that the generated answers can sometimes be inaccurate or inconsistent between different models. In addition, \textcite{Lai2025} point out that empirical evaluations of AI coaches are still limited and that the methodological quality of existing studies varies strongly. This creates the risk that models generate plausible-sounding but factually incorrect or misleading information, a phenomenon often referred to as “hallucination”.

For these reasons, the literature emphasises that LLM systems should mainly fulfil a supporting role within coaching contexts, rather than fully replacing human expertise. However, when language models are combined with reliable movement analysis and domain-specific knowledge, they can form a valuable bridge between quantitative movement data and the traditionally verbal feedback given by coaches \autocite{Jeraj2015}.

In summary, the literature suggests that LLMs are a promising technology for generating understandable feedback based on technical movement analysis. By translating output from systems such as pose estimation and automatic error detection into clear coaching instructions, such models can contribute to more accessible and structured feedback within sports training \autocite{Lai2025,He2026}. At the same time, careful validation remains necessary to ensure the reliability of generated advice.

Since research shows that the quality of a movement is strongly determined by a limited number of critical transition moments \autocite{Yamasaki2010}, it is logical to design feedback systems that focus on these key points rather than on a complete description of the movement.

\subsection{Selection of Large Language Models}
\label{subsec:selectie-llm}

Within this research, different Large Language Models (LLMs) are evaluated for their suitability for generating technical feedback based on movement analysis.

In addition to the models included in the exploratory comparison, local open-source models are also considered. Such models offer advantages in terms of privacy and local processing, but usually require more configuration and can show less consistent performance on complex language tasks. Within this research, they are therefore not used for free multimodal analysis, but are investigated as a local language component for controlled feedback generation based on already detected errors.

The selection of suitable models is based on the following criteria:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Support for multimodal input (video, image, data)
    \item Possibility of generating structured output (JSON)
    \item Consistency and reliability of the generated feedback
    \item Integration within a programmable pipeline (API/SDK)
    \item Privacy and data processing (GDPR compliance)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Privacy and GDPR considerations}

Since this research works with video footage of athletes, it is important to take privacy and data protection into account. Within the European context, such data falls under GDPR legislation.

When using external AI services, the following aspects must be taken into account:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Where and how data is processed (cloud vs local)
    \item Whether input data is stored or used for training
    \item Possibility of control over data processing
\end{itemize}

Commercial models such as Google Gemini and OpenAI GPT offer options in which data is not used for further training, depending on the configuration and API settings. This control is essential within the context of a proof-of-concept that works with potentially sensitive data.

This comparison is not intended as a comprehensive benchmark study, but as a practical justification for the choice of tools within this research. In particular, it examines the extent to which the models are suitable for (1) analysis of video data, (2) generation of structured output and (3) integration within a proof-of-concept system.

In addition to Google Gemini and OpenAI GPT-4o, alternative systems are also included in the comparison, namely Anthropic Claude and the open-source model LLaMA. This selection makes it possible to evaluate both commercial cloud-based solutions and privacy-oriented local models.

This comparison shows that Google Gemini and OpenAI GPT-4o offer the most extensive support for multimodal processing and structured output. Gemini mainly distinguishes itself through its possibilities for direct analysis of video data, which is relevant for applications within movement analysis \autocite{gemini_video}. GPT-4o, on the other hand, offers more mature and consistent support for structured output and integration within software applications \autocite{openai_json}.

Anthropic Claude forms an interesting alternative because of its focus on reliability and safety, as well as its recent support for structured output. However, the absence of native video processing makes it less suitable for applications in which video data is central \autocite{claude_vision}.

Open-source models such as LLaMA offer important advantages in terms of privacy, since processing can take place locally without data leaving external servers. This makes such models attractive from a GDPR perspective. Based on this analysis, Google Gemini is used in this research in an exploratory phase for the analysis of video input. For the further proof-of-concept, a local LLM setup through Ollama is chosen, so that it can be investigated to what extent feedback generation can be carried out locally and in a privacy-friendly way. This choice makes it possible to investigate both the possibilities of multimodal analysis and the feasibility of locally controlled feedback generation.
