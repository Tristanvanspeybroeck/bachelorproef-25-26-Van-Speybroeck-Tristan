\section{Implementation of markerless pose estimation}

The first step in the development of the proof-of-concept consisted of automatically extracting body landmarks from video footage of a gymnast performing a kip on bars. In accordance with the markerless movement analysis discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:stand-van-zaken}, the MediaPipe Pose Landmarker model was used for this purpose. This model detects anatomical keypoints of the body per frame.

In an initial implementation phase, a Python pipeline was developed that processes the video fragment frame by frame and returns the detected pose landmarks for each frame. These landmarks were first visually projected onto the original video image to check whether the gymnast was tracked consistently throughout the full movement.

The first test results showed that the global body configuration of the executing gymnast was correctly reconstructed in the majority of frames. The most important proximal joints, namely shoulders, hips and knees, remained sufficiently stable and detectable during the swing phase, hip closure and final support phase. As a result, the pose estimation proved sufficiently usable as a basis for further biomechanical analysis.

After this first validation, the visualisation was extended with a skeleton overlay in which anatomically connected landmarks were linked to each other. This skeletal representation made it possible to observe the dynamics of the body position more clearly and made it easier to check whether abrupt detection jumps actually corresponded to changes in the movement.

An important practical finding during this implementation phase was that the training video used was not a fully controlled recording. In the background, a second gymnast was also partially visible. In several frames, the pose estimation model temporarily detected body segments of both persons within the same scene, which led to disturbances in the generated landmark data. This observation confirms that markerless pose estimation remains strongly dependent on the visual clarity of the input image and is sensitive to the presence of multiple people in the camera field, as is also mentioned in the literature for practical applications outside laboratory conditions \autocite{Ota2025Markerless}.

Despite these disturbances, the pose detection over the full video fragment proved sufficiently consistent to be used further within the proof-of-concept pipeline. The visual validation confirmed that the core segments of the executing gymnast could be reconstructed to a sufficient extent, which made the next step possible, namely numerically storing this pose data and deriving biomechanically interpretable joint parameters.

\section{Extraction and storage of biomechanical parameters}

After the visual validation of the pose estimation, the pipeline was extended with a numerical extraction step in which all detected body landmarks were automatically stored frame by frame in a CSV file. For each video frame, the normalised $x$-, $y$- and $z$-coordinates were registered for each detected landmark, resulting in a full time series of consecutive body postures.

However, this raw pose data did not yet form a directly interpretable basis for technical movement analysis. To make the movement biomechanically describable, three additional derived joint parameters were implemented that correspond to the critical movement components of the kip identified in Section~\ref{sec:Biomechanische-analyse}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item hip angle (\textit{shoulder--hip--knee}),
    \item shoulder angle (\textit{elbow--shoulder--hip}),
    \item trunk angle (\textit{shoulder--hip--ankle}).
\end{itemize}

These angles were calculated per frame using vector geometry and were added to the same CSV structure together with the raw landmark coordinates. As a result, each video frame was represented not only as a collection of body positions, but also as a set of directly interpretable biomechanical parameters.

To further assess the influence of video quality and the visual clarity of the recording on the extracted angle data, the same analysis was also performed on a second video fragment in which considerably less visual disturbance was present. In contrast to the first recording, in which a second gymnast was partially visible, this second recording contained little to no disturbing background movement. This made it possible to examine to what extent the previously observed fluctuations in the angle curves were related to detection noise in the pose estimation.

Figure~\ref{fig:jointangles_comparison} compares the time course of the automatically calculated hip, shoulder and trunk angles for both video fragments. The upper graph shows the previously analysed recording with clear detection noise. Although the global movement remains recognisable, abrupt peaks, sharp drops and short jumps occur in several places that are difficult to explain biomechanically. A clear example of this is visible in the middle and final part of the time series, where several angle values suddenly change strongly over only a few consecutive frames.

The lower graph, originating from the video with little visual noise, shows a noticeably smoother course. The evolution of the hip angle, shoulder angle and trunk angle is more consistent, and large abrupt detection jumps occur much less often. This makes the movement patterns more clearly interpretable. For example, the decrease and subsequent reopening of the hip angle remain more visible, while the global evolution of the trunk angle is also less irregular.

    \label{fig:jointangles_comparison}
\end{figure}

This comparison confirms that the chosen pose estimation pipeline can extract biomechanically meaningful angle patterns from video footage. At the same time, the comparison shows that the stability of the angle curves remains strongly dependent on the visual quality of the recording. With a cleaner recording, the underlying movement pattern becomes more clearly visible, while a noisier recording causes more abrupt fluctuations.

This shows that individual frame values cannot simply be interpreted as a direct technical assessment. An isolated low or high angle value does not necessarily mean that the gymnast is making an error at that exact moment, but may also be caused by a short detection problem or inaccurate landmark localisation. For a meaningful interpretation, it is therefore necessary to take into account not only absolute angle values, but also the global evolution of these parameters over consecutive frames.

This implementation phase produced a structured numerical dataset with interpretable joint angles. Because raw angle curves do not yet form direct coaching feedback by themselves, the next step examined to what extent a Large Language Model could interpret this movement information.

\section{Exploratory experiment with existing multimodal AI analysis}

After the extraction of video footage, pose landmarks and derived joint angles, the next step investigated to what extent an existing generative AI system could independently formulate technical coaching feedback based on these data. In line with the selection of Large Language Models in Section~\ref{subsec:selectie-llm}, Google Gemini was chosen for this, since this model supports multimodal input and can interpret video material as well as graphical and numerical representations.

The goal of this experiment was not yet to build a final feedback system, but to exploratively investigate how usable the analysis of a generic multimodal AI model is when exactly the same kip execution is offered through different input forms.

Specifically, the same gymnastics execution was offered to Gemini in three separate representations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the original video fragment,
    \item the exported CSV file with pose landmarks and calculated joint angles,
    \item the generated line graph of hip, shoulder and trunk angle.
\end{itemize}

Each input was analysed in a separate session with a similar prompt in which the model was asked to technically assess the movement, identify movement phases, detect possible errors and formulate coaching feedback. The full prompts and model outputs are included in appendix ~\ref{app:gemini-experiment}.

        CSV with pose data & Detects an early arm action and insufficient hip extension & Conclusions go beyond the three available angle parameters \\

A first striking observation was that Gemini produced technically credible coaching language in all three cases without difficulty. The model used gymnastics-specific terms such as hip closure, timing of the pull, shoulder opening and body tension, making the output strongly resemble verbal coach feedback.

However, when comparing the three analyses, a clear inconsistency became visible. Based on the video fragment, Gemini concluded that the gymnast initiated the arm pull too late, while the same execution was interpreted via the CSV file as an early arm action. The graph analysis also introduced additional timing and synchronisation problems that did not fully correspond to the video analysis. Since all three outputs are based on exactly the same movement, this shows that the technical interpretation remains strongly dependent on the representation of the input data.

In addition, Gemini produced very extensive feedback in all cases, in which multiple technical errors, biomechanical explanations and additional training suggestions were discussed simultaneously. The model did not prioritise a limited number of key errors, but instead provided a full technical description of the movement each time. This aligns less well with findings from the literature on motor learning, where it is shown that targeted feedback on a limited number of key components is more effective than correcting a large number of attention points at the same time \autocite{Niznikowski2025}. For practical coaching, this implies that the generated feedback may appear rich, but can be pedagogically less efficient due to the risk of cognitive overload.

An additional observation was that Gemini formulated biomechanical conclusions that could not be directly derived from the provided data. For example, the CSV file explicitly contained only the hip angle, shoulder angle and trunk angle, while the model nevertheless generated additional statements about arm pulling force, asymmetry, momentum build-up and stabilisation. This suggests that the system inferentially fills in missing information based on learned movement patterns rather than relying solely on strictly measurable input.

This exploratory experiment therefore shows three fundamental limitations of free multimodal LLM analysis: the technical interpretation is not stable across different data forms, the generated feedback is insufficiently selectively prioritised, and additional biomechanical explanations are not always controllable based on the available measurement data.

Based on these findings, the next implementation phase focused on a more controlled analysis pipeline. First, the existing pose data extraction was technically refined, after which the movement analysis was gradually prepared for rule-based error detection and later feedback generation.

\section{Technical refinement of the pose data extraction}

After the exploratory experiment with Gemini, the existing pose data extraction was technically reworked. MediaPipe Pose Landmarker was retained as the pose estimation model, but the processing of video frames, the calculation of angle values and the storage in CSV form were refined. These adjustments had to structure the output better and make it more suitable for the further analysis pipeline.

A first adjustment concerned the timestamp registration of the frames. In the original implementation, the timestamp was increased after each frame by a fixed value of 33 milliseconds. This approximates a video of about 30 frames per second, but does not take into account the actual framerate of the loaded video file. In the revised version, the framerate is first read from the video and the timestamp per frame is calculated based on this. This better aligns with the operation of MediaPipe in video mode, where a timestamp in milliseconds is passed for each frame.

In addition, the CSV output was structured more clearly. In the first version, each row consisted only of consecutive numerical values, without column names or an explicit indication of the corresponding frame. In the reworked version, a header is added with, among other things, the frame index, the timestamp, the $x$-, $y$- and $z$-coordinates of each landmark and the three calculated angle values. This makes the output easier to read, easier to check and more practical for further processing in later phases of the system.

The way in which the angles are calculated was also technically refined. In the first implementation, the detected landmark coordinates were first rounded to integer pixel values and then used for both visualisation and calculations. In the adjusted version, this is split: rounded pixel coordinates are only used to draw points and skeleton lines on the video image, while the angle calculations are performed based on the original unrounded coordinates. In this way, more detail is preserved for the numerical processing.

Furthermore, the angle calculation itself was made more robust. Instead of deriving the angle from the difference between two directional angles, the revised version works with vectors that start from the central joint. The angle between both segments is then calculated using the dot product. In addition, a check was added for exceptional cases in which two points could coincide, as well as a clipping of the calculated cosine value to avoid numerical rounding problems. Code fragment~\ref{lst:angle_calculation_v2} shows this adjusted angle calculation.

\begin{listing}[H]
    \begin{minted}{python}
        def calculate_angle(a, b, c):
        a = np.array(a, dtype=np.float64)
        b = np.array(b, dtype=np.float64)
        c = np.array(c, dtype=np.float64)
        
        ba = a - b
        bc = c - b
        
        norm_ba = np.linalg.norm(ba)
        norm_bc = np.linalg.norm(bc)
        
        if norm_ba == 0 or norm_bc == 0:
        return np.nan
        
        cosine_angle = np.dot(ba, bc) / (norm_ba * norm_bc)
        cosine_angle = np.clip(cosine_angle, -1.0, 1.0)
        
        angle = np.degrees(np.arccos(cosine_angle))
        
        return angle
    \end{minted}
    \caption{Adjusted function for calculating joint angles based on vector geometry from \href{https://github.com/Tristanvanspeybroeck/bachelorproef-Methodologie-25-26-Van-Speybroeck-Tristan/blob/main/Methodologie/pose_project/src/pose-detection_skelet.py}{\texttt{pose-detection\_skelet.py}}}
    \label{lst:angle_calculation_v2}
\end{listing}

To examine whether this technical reworking influenced the global course of the extracted angle series, the results of the original code were compared with the results of the adjusted version. This comparison was performed for the same two video fragments used earlier: a recording with little visual noise and a recording in which the landmark detection was clearly more disturbed.

Figure~\ref{fig:code_comparison_low_noise} shows the angle curves of the recording with little visual noise before and after the code reworking. The global shape of the graphs remains very similar in both versions. The same drops, increases and transitions in hip angle, shoulder angle and trunk angle remain visible. This indicates that the technical adjustments do not change the substantive movement representation, but mainly ensure cleaner and more reliable processing of the same underlying pose data.

\section{Preparation for rule-based error detection}

After the technical refinement of the pose data extraction, the next step was taken towards a more controlled analysis of the kip movement. The exploratory Gemini experiment had shown that free multimodal interpretation did produce technically credible feedback, but was insufficiently stable and controllable to be used directly as a basis for the proof-of-concept. Therefore, it was decided to build the further analysis in a rule-based way, starting from explicitly calculated biomechanical parameters and recognisable moments in the movement.

Before the movement could be automatically divided into phases and technical errors could later be assessed, the available angle information first had to be aligned with the error types that are central in this bachelor thesis. One of these error types is bending the arms during the pull phase. To be able to analyse this quantitatively later, the existing set of joint angles was extended with a fourth parameter, the elbow angle.

\subsection{Extension of the biomechanical parameters with the elbow angle}

In the earlier implementation, three angle parameters were calculated: the hip angle, shoulder angle and trunk angle. These parameters were mainly useful for visualising global changes in body position, hip closure and shoulder movement. However, these three angles were not sufficient for the later detection of bent arms. Therefore, the elbow angle was additionally included, calculated as the angle \textit{shoulder--elbow--wrist}.

For this calculation, the already available landmarks of the right shoulder, right elbow and right wrist were used. The calculated elbow angle was then also included in the CSV output and displayed on the annotated video image, alongside the existing angle values. Code fragment~\ref{lst:elbow_angle_addition} shows how this extra angle was added to the existing processing.

\begin{listing}[H]
    \begin{minted}{python}
        right_shoulder = calc_points[12]
        right_elbow = calc_points[14]
        right_wrist = calc_points[16]
        
        elbow_angle = calculate_angle(
        right_shoulder,
        right_elbow,
        right_wrist
        )
        
        row.extend([
        hip_angle,
        shoulder_angle,
        body_angle,
        elbow_angle
        ])
    \end{minted}
    \caption{Addition of the elbow angle to the angle calculation and CSV output from \href{https://github.com/Tristanvanspeybroeck/bachelorproef-Methodologie-25-26-Van-Speybroeck-Tristan/blob/main/Methodologie/pose_project/src/pose-detection_skelet.py}{\texttt{pose-detection\_skelet.py}}}
    \label{lst:elbow_angle_addition}
\end{listing}

The extension with the elbow angle is substantively important because this parameter can later be used directly to examine to what extent the arms remain extended during the pull phase. A high elbow angle indicates a more extended arm position, while a clear decrease can indicate bending of the arm. In this phase, the elbow angle was not yet used for automatic error detection, but it was added as a necessary preparatory parameter for the further rule-based analysis.

Figure~\ref{fig:elbow_angle_comparison_clean} shows the graph of \textit{Good\_kip5} after adding the elbow angle. In this recording, the elbow angle remains high during most of the kip, after which a clearer decrease becomes visible at the end of the fragment. This decrease only occurs when the gymnast rolls further over the bar after the kip and therefore no longer belongs to the core execution that is analysed later.

\subsection{Delineation of the relevant analysis window}

For the further rule-based analysis, the recording \textit{Good\_kip5} was used, since this video contained considerably less detection noise than the previously discussed recording with a second gymnast in view. This made this execution more suitable for developing the first phase division and later error detection in a controllable way.

However, the full video contained more than only the core execution of the kip. During visual inspection of the annotated video, in which the current frame number was also displayed, it was determined that the relevant kip movement starts around frame 80. Before this moment, the gymnast is still in the preparatory movement towards the bar. From frame 171 onwards, the gymnast rolls further over the bar, which no longer belongs to the actual kip movement analysed within this research.

To limit the further processing to the technically relevant fragment, an analysis window was therefore defined from frame 80 up to and including frame 170. Within this range, only the execution of the kip itself is considered, from the start of the relevant swing movement to just before the additional roll after reaching the support position.

Code fragment~\ref{lst:analysis_window_selection} shows how this analysis window was selected from the CSV data.

\begin{listing}[H]
    \begin{minted}{python}
        START_FRAME = 80
        END_FRAME = 170
        
        analysis_data = data[
        (data["frame_index"] >= START_FRAME) &
        (data["frame_index"] <= END_FRAME)
        ].copy()
    \end{minted}
    \caption{Selection of the relevant analysis window within the CSV data from \href{https://github.com/Tristanvanspeybroeck/bachelorproef-Methodologie-25-26-Van-Speybroeck-Tristan/blob/main/Methodologie/pose_project/src/analyze_kip_execution.py}{\texttt{analyze\_kip\_execution.py}}}
    \label{lst:analysis_window_selection}
\end{listing}

The selected range theoretically contains 91 video frames. In the CSV data, 86 measurement rows were available within this analysis window. This difference is due to the fact that MediaPipe did not store usable pose output for every video frame. Within this proof-of-concept, missing frames were not interpolated, because this would add additional estimations on top of the pose estimation. The further analysis was therefore performed on the available valid measurement rows within the chosen frame range.

Figure~\ref{fig:analysis_window_goodkip5} shows the course of the four calculated joint angles within this selected analysis window. By showing only the relevant part of the movement, the changes in hip angle, shoulder angle, trunk angle and elbow angle become more clearly visible than in the original graph over the full video. This delineation therefore forms a necessary preparatory step for automatically detecting critical moments and dividing the movement into separate phases.

\subsection{Automatic detection of critical anchor points}

After delineating the relevant analysis window, it was investigated whether several critical moments within the kip movement could be automatically derived from the angle data. These anchor points are needed to subsequently divide the movement into phases in a reproducible way. In this step, no technical error detection was performed yet. The goal was first to automatically locate recognisable transition moments in the movement.

A first important anchor point is the moment of maximum hip closure. This moment is biomechanically relevant because during the kip, the gymnast brings the legs and trunk close to the bar to move the body efficiently towards the support position. Within the angle data, this moment was approximated by searching for the frame on which the hip angle is minimal. As a check, the frame on which the trunk angle is minimal was also determined.

Code fragment~\ref{lst:hip_closure_detection} shows how these minimum angle values were determined from the selected analysis window.

\begin{listing}[H]
    \begin{minted}{python}
        hip_closure_index = analysis_data["hip_angle"].idxmin()
        hip_closure_frame = int(
        analysis_data.loc[hip_closure_index, "frame_index"]
        )
        hip_closure_angle = analysis_data.loc[
        hip_closure_index,
        "hip_angle"
        ]
        
        body_closure_index = analysis_data["body_angle"].idxmin()
        body_closure_frame = int(
        analysis_data.loc[body_closure_index, "frame_index"]
        )
        body_closure_angle = analysis_data.loc[
        body_closure_index,
        "body_angle"
        ]
    \end{minted}
    \caption{Automatic detection of the moment of maximum hip and trunk closure from \href{https://github.com/Tristanvanspeybroeck/bachelorproef-Methodologie-25-26-Van-Speybroeck-Tristan/blob/main/Methodologie/pose_project/src/analyze_kip_execution.py}{\texttt{analyze\_kip\_execution.py}}}
    \label{lst:hip_closure_detection}
\end{listing}

Within the selected analysis window, the minimum hip angle was found at frame 125, with a value of 65.52°. The minimum trunk angle was found almost simultaneously at frame 124, with a value of 65.63°. The proximity of these two frames confirms that both parameters describe the same global closing moment in the movement. During visual inspection of the video, frame 125 appeared to correspond to the moment at which the gymnast's shin comes close to the bar, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:frame125_hip_closure}. This supports the interpretation that the minimum hip angle in this recording forms a usable automatic anchor point for the hip closure phase.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.45\linewidth]{good_kip5_frame125_hip_closure.png}
    \caption{Visual check of frame 125, the automatically detected moment of maximum hip closure. (video: Good\_kip5)}
    \label{fig:frame125_hip_closure}
\end{figure}

In addition to maximum hip closure, the start of the support phase was also automatically approximated. After hip closure, the body opens again and the gymnast moves towards the support position above the bar. To detect this moment, a simple rule was defined. The support phase begins when both the hip angle and trunk angle are at least 160° for several consecutive measurement rows. By requiring three consecutive measurement rows, it is avoided that one coincidental peak or short noisy frame is incorrectly interpreted as the start of the support phase.

In this recording, the start of the support phase was automatically found at frame 145. This is very close to the earlier visual estimate, in which the first clear support position was observed around frame 144. As a result, this simple threshold rule proved sufficiently usable to locate the start of the support phase in this execution.

Finally, the start of the pull phase was also investigated. For this, a simultaneous decrease in the shoulder angle and hip angle before the moment of maximum hip closure was examined. The reasoning here is that the transition from the swing movement to the active pulling and closing movement is accompanied by a clear change in both angles. To avoid interpreting a small frame-to-frame fluctuation as a starting point, the analysis did not look at a single difference, but at a decrease over multiple measurement rows.

The rule used compares the current angle values with the values three measurement rows earlier. When both the shoulder angle and hip angle decrease by at least 10° over that period and this occurs for two consecutive measurement rows, this is considered the start of the pull phase. Based on this rule, the start of the pull phase was automatically found at frame 117.

Figure~\ref{fig:anchor_points_goodkip5} shows the four angle curves within the analysis window, supplemented with vertical markings for the automatically found anchor points. This shows that the detected moments logically coincide with clear changes in the course of the angle curves. The start of the pull phase is located shortly before maximum hip closure, while the start of the support phase follows after the hip angle and trunk angle increase strongly again.

\subsection{Rule-based delineation of the movement phases}

Based on the automatically found anchor points, the kip movement was then operationally divided into separate movement phases. This phase division is necessary because later error detection cannot be applied to the full movement at once. Each technical error is linked to a specific part of the execution. For example, the elbow angle is mainly relevant during the pull phase, while the hip angle is mainly important around the hip closure phase and the subsequent opening.

For this first rule-based delineation, the previously detected start of the pull phase, the moment of maximum hip closure and the start of the support phase were used as the starting point. Maximum hip closure at frame 125 was used as the central anchor point. Around this frame, a small window of three frames before and three frames after the closing moment was taken, so that the hip closure phase was not limited to one single frame, but could be analysed as a short movement fragment.

Based on this, four movement phases were delineated. The swing phase runs from the start of the analysis window to just before the automatically detected start of the pull phase. The pull phase starts at frame 117 and runs until just before the window around maximum hip closure. The hip closure phase is delineated around frame 125, while the support phase starts at frame 145 and runs until the end of the analysis window.

In addition to these four formal movement phases, an additional internal analysis window was also provided between the end of the hip closure phase and the start of the support phase. This transition window runs from frame 129 up to and including frame 144 and contains 14 available measurement rows. It is not included as a separate fifth phase in the movement description, but serves as a technical window for the later analysis of possible early hip opening. In this way, the global phase division remains limited to the four previously described phases, while the code still preserves enough information to assess this specific error later.

Figure~\ref{fig:phase_detection_goodkip5} shows the final phase division on the angle graph. The visualisation shows that the delineated phases logically coincide with the course of the angle curves. The movement is no longer considered only as one complete time series, but as a series of meaningful movement fragments. As a result, later error detection can be carried out more specifically per phase. The elbow angle can be assessed during the pull phase, the hip angle during the hip closure phase, the speed or timing of hip opening in the transition window and the body posture during the support phase. The actual implementation of these error rules forms the next development step of the system.

\section{Iterative refinement of the rule-based analysis}

The phase division based on the clean execution \textit{Good\_kip5} formed a first usable basis for the further error detection. However, when applying the same analysis to additional attempts, it became clear that the rules did not work equally stably on every video without refinement. Especially short noise peaks in the angle curves could influence the automatically found anchor points and therefore also the subsequent phase division.

Therefore, the rule-based analysis was iteratively adjusted. These adjustments did not aim to replace the underlying pose estimation model, but to interpret the existing output more robustly. The most important refinements related to the detection of key moments, the delineation of movement phases and the rules by which technical errors are determined.

\subsection{Refinement of anchor point detection with smoothing}

In the first version of the analysis, key moments, such as maximum hip closure, were determined directly based on the raw angle values. This worked sufficiently well for the clean execution, but proved more sensitive in attempts where the angle curves showed short outliers. In one of the incorrect attempts, a short noise peak caused a less representative frame to be selected as maximum hip closure.

To reduce this, a simple smoothing step was added. For the hip angle and trunk angle, a rolling median with a window of five measurement rows was calculated. These filtered series are used to determine the most important anchor points, such as minimum hip angle and minimum trunk angle, more stably. The original raw angle values are still retained for further analysis, visualisation and reporting.

\begin{listing}[H]
    \begin{minted}{python}
        SMOOTHING_WINDOW = 5
        
        analysis_data["hip_angle_smooth"] = (
        analysis_data["hip_angle"]
        .rolling(
        window=SMOOTHING_WINDOW,
        center=True,
        min_periods=1
        )
        .median()
        )
        
        analysis_data["body_angle_smooth"] = (
        analysis_data["body_angle"]
        .rolling(
        window=SMOOTHING_WINDOW,
        center=True,
        min_periods=1
        )
        .median()
        )
    \end{minted}
    \caption{Application of a rolling median to the hip angle and trunk angle for more stable anchor point detection from \href{https://github.com/Tristanvanspeybroeck/bachelorproef-Methodologie-25-26-Van-Speybroeck-Tristan/blob/main/Methodologie/pose_project/src/analyze_kip_execution.py}{\texttt{analyze\_kip\_execution.py}}}
    \label{lst:smoothing_anchor_points}
\end{listing}

Figure~\ref{fig:badkip2_smoothing} shows the effect of this adjustment for an incorrect attempt. The raw angle curves remain visible, but the filtered hip angle makes it possible to make maximum hip closure less dependent on one individual outlier. As a result, the central anchor point for the hip closure phase is chosen more stably and the subsequent phase division becomes more consistent.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{Bad_kip2_analyze_with_smoothing_hip_angle.png}
    \caption{Use of a filtered hip angle to determine the moment of maximum hip closure more stably in an incorrect attempt. (video: Bad\_kip2)}
    \label{fig:badkip2_smoothing}
\end{figure}

This smoothing step does not completely solve the detection noise, but reduces the influence of short, isolated measurement deviations on the choice of anchor points. This is important because the further error detection starts from the automatically delineated phases. When the anchor point for hip closure shifts due to one noise peak, the pull phase, hip closure phase and transition to the support phase also shift. By lightly filtering the anchor point detection, the analysis pipeline becomes more robust without fully replacing the raw movement data.

\subsection{Refinement of the error rules}

In addition to the detection of anchor points, the error rules themselves were also iteratively refined. The first version of the rule-based analysis was sufficient to technically implement the different error types, but tests on multiple attempts showed that some rules were too sensitive or were biomechanically formulated too simply. Therefore, the rules were adjusted based on the combination of measurement values, graphical inspection and visual inspection of the video footage.

A first important refinement related to the detection of \textit{Early hip opening}. In an earlier version, a rapid increase in the hip angle after hip closure was interpreted by itself as a possible error. Upon closer inspection, this reasoning proved too simplified. During a correct kip, the hip must open again after hip closure in order to move towards the support position. A rapid hip opening is therefore not automatically incorrect.

Therefore, the rule was adjusted. In the final version, \textit{Early hip opening} is only detected when the hip is already strongly opened while the global body alignment is still insufficient, and this for several consecutive measurement rows. In this way, the normal opening after hip closure is not penalised, but only an opening that occurs too early in relation to the position of the body.

\begin{listing}[H]
    \begin{minted}{python}
        early_hip_opening_problem_rows = (
        (
        hip_opening_transition_data["hip_angle_smooth"] >
        EARLY_HIP_OPENING_HIP_THRESHOLD
        ) &
        (
        hip_opening_transition_data["body_angle_smooth"] <
        EARLY_HIP_OPENING_BODY_THRESHOLD
        )
        ).sum()
        
        early_hip_opening_detected = (
        early_hip_opening_problem_rows >=
        EARLY_HIP_OPENING_MIN_ROWS
        )
    \end{minted}
    \caption{Refined detection rule for early hip opening from \href{https://github.com/Tristanvanspeybroeck/bachelorproef-Methodologie-25-26-Van-Speybroeck-Tristan/blob/main/Methodologie/pose_project/src/analyze_kip_execution.py}{\texttt{analyze\_kip\_execution.py}}}
    \label{lst:early_hip_opening_rule}
\end{listing}

A second important refinement related to \textit{Weak shoulder push}. In the first version, this error was detected when more than five percent of the support phase rows showed an elbow angle below 160°. However, this rule proved too sensitive for the good execution \textit{Good\_kip5}. In that attempt, only two short support phase rows fell below this threshold, but because the support phase contained relatively few measurement rows, this was already sufficient in percentage terms to activate the error. This resulted in a false positive.

To avoid this, the rule was extended with an additional minimum condition. In the final version, not only must the proportion of deviating support phase rows be above five percent, but there must also be at least three support phase rows with an elbow angle below 160°. As a result, very short deviations are less quickly interpreted as a technical error.

\begin{listing}[H]
    \begin{minted}{python}
        weak_shoulder_push_detected = (
        support_body_angle_mean < WEAK_SUPPORT_BODY_ANGLE_THRESHOLD or
        support_elbow_angle_mean < WEAK_SUPPORT_ELBOW_MEAN_THRESHOLD or
        (
        support_bent_elbow_rows >=
        WEAK_SUPPORT_BENT_ELBOW_MIN_ROWS and
        support_bent_elbow_ratio >
        WEAK_SUPPORT_BENT_ELBOW_RATIO_THRESHOLD
        ) or
        support_shoulder_angle_mean >
        WEAK_SUPPORT_SHOULDER_ANGLE_THRESHOLD
        )
    \end{minted}
    \caption{Refined detection rule for weak shoulder push in the support phase from \href{https://github.com/Tristanvanspeybroeck/bachelorproef-Methodologie-25-26-Van-Speybroeck-Tristan/blob/main/Methodologie/pose_project/src/analyze_kip_execution.py}{\texttt{analyze\_kip\_execution.py}}}
    \label{lst:weak_shoulder_push_rule}
\end{listing}

The adjustment to the rule for \textit{Weak shoulder push} was checked on the three main test attempts. For \textit{Good\_kip5}, the earlier false positive was resolved: the two short deviating support phase rows were no longer sufficient to activate the error. For \textit{Bad\_kip1}, the error remained present, because three support phase rows fell below the threshold. For \textit{Bad\_kip2}, the error also remained clearly detected, since a much larger part of the support phase showed deviating elbow values.

These refinements show that the chosen rule-based approach is controllable and adjustable, but also remains sensitive to the chosen threshold values. The rules were therefore not considered definitively validated gymnastics assessment criteria, but as explicit and reproducible decision rules within the proof-of-concept. This makes it possible to transparently follow the operation of the system and to adjust it in a targeted way when a rule proves too sensitive or too general.

\subsection{Structuring the analysis output in JSON}

After the refinement of the error rules, the output of the analysis was stored in a structured JSON format. This step was necessary because the console output was useful during development and testing, but less suitable as an interface between the rule-based analysis and the feedback generation. By storing the results as JSON, it is clearly recorded which phases were recognised, which measurement values were used, which rules were applied and which errors were ultimately detected.

The JSON output contains both technical analysis information and a limited task description for the language model. The technical information remains necessary to check the operation of the system and to be able to determine afterwards why a specific error was or was not detected. At the same time, the JSON also explicitly indicates that the LLM may not perform new error detection, but may only convert the already detected errors into understandable feedback.

For each analysed attempt, the analysis window, movement phases, key moments, used joint angles, relevant measurement values per phase and applied detection rules are stored, among other things. In addition, the JSON contains a list of all checked error types and a separate list containing only the errors that were actually detected. This distinction is important, because it allows the system to keep track of which errors were checked but not observed, without these automatically being passed on as feedback to the gymnast.

\section{Final test results of the error detection}

After the iterative refinement of the anchor point detection, error rules and JSON output, the final version of the rule-based analysis was tested on multiple attempts. This deliberately included both a good execution and incorrect executions. In this way, it could be examined whether the system does not detect unjustified errors in a correct attempt, while still retaining the expected errors in less good executions.

The test set within this proof-of-concept remained limited. The results should therefore not be interpreted as a formal validation of the error detection, but as a check of the technical operation of the developed pipeline. For each attempt, the relevant analysis window was set manually, after which the analysis automatically generated the phases, measurement values, error detections and JSON output.

The pull phase ran from frame 117 up to and including frame 120, the hip closure phase from frame 121 up to and including frame 127, and the support phase from frame 145 up to and including frame 170. \section{Feedback generation with a local LLM}

After the rule-based error detection, it was investigated whether the structured analysis output could be automatically converted into understandable coaching feedback. In contrast to the earlier exploratory experiment with Gemini, the language model in this phase was no longer asked to detect technical errors itself. The error detection had already been performed by the Python script. The role of the LLM was therefore limited to verbalising the actually detected errors into understandable feedback for a recreational gymnast and coach.

For this step, a local language model through Ollama was used. This choice aligned with the proof-of-concept, because the feedback generation could be performed locally without sending the analysis data to an external cloud service. In the final test, the model \texttt{qwen2.5:7b} was used. The choice of this model was not intended as a definitive model comparison, but as a practical configuration to investigate whether local feedback generation was technically feasible.

\subsection{Prompt iterations and model choice}

The first prompt iterations were intended to investigate how well a local language model could convert the JSON output of the analysis into coaching feedback. In doing so, the model was explicitly instructed to work only with the errors that had been detected by the rule-based system. The model was therefore not allowed to perform new error detection, discuss non-detected errors or add errors outside the scope of the proof-of-concept.

During the first tests, it became clear that this delineation was necessary. Although the LLM generally aligned substantively with the detected errors, the model sometimes adopted English error names or technical terms from the JSON in the Dutch feedback. In addition, unnatural formulations sometimes occurred, such as literal translations of English phase names. This made clear that not only the JSON content, but also the prompt language and the labels used had an influence on the final feedback quality.

Therefore, the prompts were iteratively tightened. In later versions, it was more strongly emphasised that the model was not allowed to show raw measurement values, threshold values, file names, JSON fields or English terms to the user. The desired output structure was also defined more explicitly: a short general assessment, a list of the most important attention points, concrete coaching tips and a short positive closing. This structure had to prevent the model from generating a technical report instead of usable coaching feedback.

An important choice in this phase was the transition to a fully Dutch prompt. The earlier English prompt versions could produce substantively usable feedback, but increased the chance that English terms from the JSON would appear literally in the output. Because the intended context of this bachelor thesis is a Dutch-speaking Belgian training environment, the work ultimately continued with a Dutch prompt version.

Table~\ref{tab:prompt_iterations} summarises the most important prompt iterations.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{p{2.4cm}p{4.2cm}p{5.8cm}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Prompt version} & \textbf{Goal} & \textbf{Main observation} \\
        \midrule

This choice produced usable results within the current proof-of-concept, but the feedback was not completely flawless. The model could convert the detected errors into understandable text, but sometimes continued to use general or unnatural formulations. Therefore, the LLM output in this bachelor thesis is considered a technical demonstration of automatic feedback generation, not fully validated coaching feedback.

\subsection{Filtered LLM input and zero-error logic}

After the prompt iterations, the input to the LLM was also further limited. The full JSON output of the analysis contains more information than is needed for feedback generation. In addition to the actually detected errors, this JSON also contains the full list of checked error types, the detection rules, threshold values, measurement values and non-detected errors. When this full JSON was provided directly to the language model, there was a risk that the LLM would also start discussing non-detected errors or technical details.

Therefore, a limited JSON structure was built before the LLM call. This structure contains only the information that is relevant for feedback generation: the exercise, the analysis window, the movement phases, the actually detected errors, the number of detected errors, the task description for the LLM and several general notes. The components \texttt{error\_checks} and \texttt{detection\_rules} are deliberately not passed on to the LLM, because this information mainly serves technical control and not the formulation of feedback.

Code fragment~\ref{lst:llm_filtered_input} shows how this filtered input was built.

            "detected_error_types": analysis_result.get(
            "detected_error_types", []
            ),
            "detected_error_count": analysis_result.get(
            "detected_error_count", 0
            ),
            "llm_task": analysis_result.get("llm_task"),
            "notes": analysis_result.get("notes")
        }

    \label{lst:llm_filtered_input}

This filtering strengthens the division of tasks within the pipeline. The Python script remains responsible for the error detection, while the LLM may only convert the detected errors into natural language. This prevents the language model from drawing its own conclusions based on technical intermediate information or mentioning errors that were not activated by the rule-based system.

In addition, zero-error logic was added. When \texttt{detected\_error\_count} equals zero, the LLM is not called. In that case, the script directly generates a fixed neutral message. This choice was made because language models can have a tendency to formulate general improvement points or attention points even when the analysis is error-free. For a feedback system, this is undesirable, because a correct execution would then still receive unnecessary corrective feedback.

This logic was applied to the good execution \textit{Good\_kip5}. Since the final error detection for this attempt produced no errors, no LLM feedback was generated. Instead, the fixed message was shown stating that, within the current proof-of-concept, no technical errors were detected for the checked error types. This aligns with the goal of avoiding unnecessary or invented feedback.

For the incorrect attempts, where errors were detected, the filtered JSON input was passed on to the local language model. The LLM was given access only to the actually detected errors and the corresponding context. In this way, the chance was reduced that the model would give feedback about errors that had not been identified by the rule-based system.

\subsection{Quality and limitations of the generated feedback}

After adding the filtered LLM input and the zero-error logic, the feedback generation was tested on the three final attempts. For \textit{Good\_kip5}, no errors were detected, so the LLM was not called. In that case, the system only gave the fixed message that, within the current proof-of-concept, no technical errors were found for the checked error types. This result was desired, because unnecessary corrective feedback was avoided.

For \textit{Bad\_kip1}, the LLM was called, since the rule-based system detected two errors: \textit{Bent arms} and \textit{Weak shoulder push}. The generated feedback aligned substantively with these two errors. The model indicated that the arms did not remain sufficiently extended during the pull phase and that the support phase was not finished powerfully or stably enough. In this way, the core of the JSON analysis was correctly converted into understandable coaching feedback.

Feedback was also generated for \textit{Bad\_kip2} based on the filtered JSON input. In this attempt, only \textit{Weak shoulder push} was detected. The LLM therefore limited the feedback to the support phase and did not formulate additional remarks about bent arms, insufficient hip closure or early hip opening. This confirms that filtering the input helps to better align the feedback with the errors that were actually detected.

The results show that the LLM is usable as a language component on top of the rule-based analysis. The error detection is performed by the Python script and the LLM only translates these results into a more understandable form. As a result, the pipeline remains more controllable than in the earlier Gemini experiment.

Nevertheless, the quality of the generated feedback remained limited. Despite the stricter prompt and the filtered input, general formulations or less natural sentences still occurred at times. The prompt also had to explicitly prohibit the adoption of English terms, measurement values, JSON fields or technical variables. This shows that the LLM output cannot be considered finished coaching feedback without review.

The feedback generation therefore mainly proves that automatic textual feedback is technically possible within the developed pipeline. The combination of rule-based error detection and a local LLM makes it possible to convert measurable errors into Dutch-language feedback. Within this bachelor thesis, however, this remains a proof-of-concept. For practical use in a training context, the feedback would have to be further evaluated by coaches and tested on a larger variation of executions.
