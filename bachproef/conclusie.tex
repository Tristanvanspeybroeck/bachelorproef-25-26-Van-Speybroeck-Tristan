This research investigated to what extent an AI-based system using markerless video analysis can generate technically useful feedback for recreational gymnasts performing the kip on bars. For this purpose, a proof-of-concept was developed in which video footage is first converted into pose data, then into biomechanical angle parameters, then into rule-based error detection and finally into textual feedback through a Large Language Model.

The central conclusion is that such an approach is technically feasible, but within the current implementation it is not yet sufficiently reliable as an independent coaching system. The proof-of-concept shows that markerless pose estimation, in this case through MediaPipe, can produce usable movement data from standard video footage. Based on these data, relevant joint angles could be calculated and predefined technical errors could be detected using a rule-based approach. At the same time, the analysis proved to remain sensitive to detection noise, background movement and the chosen threshold values.

\section{Answering the research questions}

\subsection{Main research question}

\textit{To what extent can an AI-based system using markerless video analysis generate technically useful feedback for recreational gymnasts performing the kip on bars?}

The main research question can be answered positively to a limited extent. The developed proof-of-concept shows that automatic feedback is technically possible, but only when the error detection is controlled and rule-based. However, the approach remains limited to an exploratory proof-of-concept.

\subsection{Sub-question 1}

\textit{Which technical errors occur most frequently during the kip?}

From the literature and the practical scope of this research, four relevant error types emerged: bent arms, insufficient hip closure, early hip opening and weak shoulder push. These errors correspond to important movement components of the kip, namely swing momentum, hip closure, arm extension and active shoulder action.

\subsection{Sub-question 2}

\textit{Why are recurring technical errors sometimes not noticed by coaches, and how can this be systematically recorded?}

In recreational training contexts, coaches often supervise several gymnasts at the same time and must visually assess fast-moving, complex movements. As a result, it is difficult to analyse every execution consistently and objectively. Within this research, it was shown that pose data and calculated angle curves can help to record technical aspects of the execution more systematically. In doing so, the analysis does not replace the coach, but can serve as additional support.

\subsection{Sub-question 3}

\textit{Which characteristics make technical feedback effective and useful for recreational gymnasts?}

The literature shows that feedback should mainly be targeted, understandable and limited to the most important attention points. Too much error correction at the same time can be cognitively demanding. Therefore, in the proof-of-concept, feedback was only generated about errors that were actually detected by the rule-based system.

\subsection{Sub-question 4}

\textit{Which markerless pose estimation method is most suitable for analysing the kip on bars within a recreational training context?}

Within this research, MediaPipe Pose Landmarker was chosen because this system enables markerless pose estimation based on standard video footage. The system does not require markers or specialised measurement equipment, which fits the recreational context of this research. The results show that MediaPipe can produce usable movement data, but that the quality remains dependent on recording quality, camera angle, background movements and detection noise.

\subsection{Sub-question 5}

\textit{To what extent can technical errors be automatically detected based on pose data?}

Automatic error detection based on pose data proved possible within the limited test set. In the good execution, \textit{Good\_kip5}, no errors were detected after refinement. In \textit{Bad\_kip1}, bent arms in the pull phase and weak shoulder push in the support phase were identified. In \textit{Bad\_kip2}, mainly weak shoulder push in the support phase was detected. These results show that the rule-based approach can recognise relevant differences between attempts. At the same time, they should not be interpreted as a formal validation, because only a limited set of video attempts was tested.

\subsection{Sub-question 6}

\textit{To what extent can a Large Language Model provide technically correct and useful feedback based on detected errors?}

The LLM was able to convert detected errors into short Dutch-language feedback, but the quality varied. In some cases, the feedback remained too general, the model used English error names or it formulated unnatural coaching sentences. In addition, it became clear that an LLM may tend to invent additional improvement points when it receives too much information or when no errors have been detected. Therefore, the role of the LLM in the final pipeline was deliberately limited. The LLM does not detect errors, but only verbalises the errors that were already identified by the rule-based system.

\subsection{Sub-question 7}

\textit{What are the limitations of an AI-based feedback system within this application?}

The main limitations relate to the limited test set, the sensitivity of 2D pose estimation to noise and camera angle, and the need for strict control of the LLM output. Not all error types were positively identified within the available test set either.

\section{Reflection on the result}

The result of this research is in line with expectations, but also clearly shows the limits of the current technology and implementation. AI can certainly offer support for technical analysis in gymnastics, but not in a way where a language model independently assesses a movement. The exploratory experiment with Gemini showed that a multimodal model can formulate technically credible feedback, but also that this feedback depends on the input form and is not always controllable based on the available measurement data.

The main added value of the proof-of-concept therefore lies not in the LLM as an evaluator, but in the combination of pose estimation, rule-based analysis and controlled feedback generation. MediaPipe functions as the AI component for extracting body positions from video. The rule-based analysis provides controllable error detection. The LLM is only used afterwards as a language component to formulate technical findings in a more understandable way.

During the development, it also became clear that a negative or limited outcome can still be valuable. The LLM feedback was not always good enough to be used directly as final coaching feedback. This is an important result in itself: without strict delineation, an LLM can give vague feedback or interpret technical elements that were not identified by the analysis. This finding led to a more robust pipeline in which the LLM input is filtered and in which no LLM call is made when there are no errors.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:beperkingen}

The main limitation of this research is the limited test set. The error rules were tested on a small number of attempts and are therefore not statistically validated. The threshold values used are based on iterative development, visual inspection and the available test videos. Due to this limited and unlabelled dataset, no data-driven machine learning model was trained for error detection within this research. A rule-based approach was more controllable in this context, because each detection remains linked to explicit measurement values and threshold values.

A second limitation is that the analysis is based on 2D pose estimation. As a result, perspective distortion, camera angle and temporarily occluded body parts can influence the calculated angles. Especially in gymnastics, where movements are fast and body parts can temporarily overlap, this can lead to noise in the landmark detection. The added smoothing partly reduces this problem, but does not solve it completely.

A third limitation is that the analysis window was chosen manually. This was acceptable within the proof-of-concept, but for a fully automatic system, the start and end of the relevant kip movement would also have to be determined automatically.

In addition, some detection rules still work based on numbers of measurement rows instead of explicit time durations, which means that further time normalisation is needed when using video files with varying framerates.

Finally, feedback generation through a local LLM remains limited. The choice for local processing aligns with the privacy and GDPR considerations within this research, because video data and analysis data do not have to be sent to an external cloud service. However, the language output of the model used was less consistent than desired. More powerful or better adapted models may possibly provide better feedback, but this must be further investigated in future research.

\section{Future work}

Based on this proof-of-concept, several extensions are possible. A first logical next step is testing on a larger dataset with multiple gymnasts, different camera angles and more variation in execution quality. This would allow the threshold values for error detection to be evaluated and refined more systematically.

In addition, future research can extend the analysis with explicit via-points, angular velocities or methods such as Dynamic Time Warping. In this research, phases and angle thresholds were used, but the literature shows that the kip strongly depends on critical transition moments. A system that directly detects these via-points could possibly deal better with timing errors and individual variation in execution.

Feedback generation can also be investigated further. A more powerful language model or a model with more domain-specific instructions could possibly provide more natural and more concrete feedback. In addition, it can be investigated whether a domain-specific trained or fine-tuned model can interpret the JSON output, the technical error types and the kip movement better than the generally deployable language models tested in this proof-of-concept. It remains important, however, that the language model does not perform autonomous error detection without reliable analysis output. A comparison between local models and commercial models may be interesting in this regard, provided that privacy and data processing are managed correctly.

Finally, a future version of the system could be made more user-friendly for gymnasts and coaches. This could include a simple interface in which a video is uploaded, the phase division is visible, the detected errors are displayed and the generated feedback can be checked by the coach.

\section{Final conclusion}

This research shows that automatic technical feedback on the kip on bars is feasible to a limited extent through a combination of markerless pose estimation, rule-based error detection and LLM-based text generation. The proof-of-concept succeeds in converting standard video footage into pose data, calculating relevant joint angles, detecting several predefined errors and converting these errors into understandable feedback.

At the same time, the research shows that this approach still has clear limitations. Pose estimation remains sensitive to noise, error detection depends on threshold values and the LLM output is not reliable enough without strict control. The system should therefore mainly be considered as exploratory support for coaches, not as a replacement for human expertise.

The contribution of this research lies in developing and critically evaluating a complete analysis pipeline. The results form a useful basis on which further research can build, for example with larger datasets, better error rules, explicit via-point detection and more powerful or better controlled feedback models.
